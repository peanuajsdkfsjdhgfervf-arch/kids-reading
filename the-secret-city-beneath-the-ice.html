The Secret City Beneath the Ice

Imagine walking across a giant sheet of ice in Greenland.

There are no trees. No roads. No houses. Almost everything you can see is white.

Now imagine that underneath your boots—buried beneath more than 30 metres of snow and ice—is an abandoned city.

Not an ancient city.

A modern one.

It had bedrooms, kitchens, laboratories, machines, tunnels and even a nuclear reactor.

And for years, almost nobody outside the project knew exactly what was happening there.

Its name was Camp Century.

Building a City Inside a Glacier

In 1959, engineers from the United States Army travelled deep into Greenland.

They wanted to find out whether people could live and work underneath the enormous Greenland ice sheet.

Instead of building on top of the ice, they dug down into it.

Huge machines carved long trenches through the snow. Workers then covered the trenches with curved metal roofs and buried everything again.

Before long, an underground network began to appear.

Camp Century eventually contained roughly three kilometres of tunnels.

Inside were sleeping quarters, offices, a kitchen, a dining hall, laboratories, showers and equipment rooms.

There was even a small store.

Imagine waking up in the morning, getting dressed and walking to breakfast without ever seeing the sky.

Every hallway around you would be made of snow and ice.

Electricity in the Middle of Nowhere

Running a town in one of the coldest places on Earth requires a lot of energy.

Normally, remote bases need huge amounts of diesel fuel.

But transporting fuel across Greenland was difficult and expensive.

So engineers tried something much more unusual.

They installed a small portable nuclear reactor.

The reactor produced electricity and heat for the base.

That meant Camp Century became one of the strangest communities ever built: a nuclear-powered town buried inside a glacier.

For the engineers working there, it was also an enormous experiment.

They studied how buildings behaved inside moving ice, how machines operated in extreme cold and how people handled living underground for long periods.

The Problem Nobody Could Stop

There was one major problem.

A glacier looks solid.

But it isn't completely still.

Ice slowly flows.

Think about squeezing toothpaste from a tube, except incredibly slowly.

The enormous weight of Greenland's ice sheet causes the ice underneath to gradually move outward.

Camp Century's engineers knew the ice moved.

What they didn't fully realize was how quickly that movement would affect their tunnels.

The walls began pushing inward.

Ceilings started dropping.

Every year, workers had to cut away more ice just to keep the tunnels open.

Some areas became crooked.

Others became dangerous.

Imagine building a perfectly straight hallway and discovering a few years later that the walls were slowly trying to crush it.

No machine could stop the entire Greenland ice sheet from moving.

A Secret Plan

Camp Century also had another purpose.

The military studied whether the Greenland ice sheet could someday hide a giant network of missile-launching sites.

The idea was called Project Iceworm.

Engineers imagined thousands of kilometres of tunnels underneath the ice.

Missiles could potentially be moved around so an enemy would not know exactly where they were located.

On paper, it sounded clever.

But Camp Century revealed a serious problem.

If the ice was constantly shifting, giant underground missile tunnels would bend, shrink and eventually collapse.

Project Iceworm was never built.

Leaving the City Behind

Camp Century operated for several years.

Eventually, the nuclear reactor was removed and the base was abandoned.

The engineers shut the doors and left.

Snow continued falling.

The ice continued moving.

Slowly, Camp Century disappeared.

Today, the base is buried far beneath the surface.

Its tunnels have probably been badly crushed and distorted by decades of moving ice.

There is no easy doorway that explorers can simply dig out and enter.

The glacier has swallowed the city.

A Message From the Past

Camp Century did leave behind something extremely valuable.

Scientists drilled deep into the ice beneath the base and collected long cylinders called ice cores.

Ice cores are like time machines.

Each layer contains clues about what Earth's atmosphere and climate were like thousands of years ago.

Scientists can examine tiny bubbles trapped inside the ice.

Those bubbles contain ancient air.

By studying them, researchers can learn about temperatures, volcanic eruptions and changes in Earth's atmosphere.

Even more surprisingly, researchers later examined material taken from beneath Greenland's ice.

They found evidence that parts of Greenland were once much warmer and may once have supported plants.

That discovery matters because Greenland currently contains enough frozen water to raise global sea levels dramatically if the entire ice sheet were ever to melt.

Nobody expects that to happen suddenly.

But understanding Greenland's past helps scientists predict its future.

Would You Explore It?

Imagine scientists invented a machine capable of safely drilling a tunnel down to the remains of Camp Century.

You are invited on the first expedition.

You climb into a small elevator.

The doors close.

You begin descending through decades of snow and ice.

Ten metres.

Twenty metres.

Thirty metres.

The light above disappears.

Eventually, your elevator reaches an old tunnel.

Your flashlight shines across twisted steel supports sticking from the ice.

Farther ahead you see the remains of a doorway.

A faded sign is frozen into the wall.

You have two choices.

One tunnel leads toward the old laboratories.

The other leads toward the abandoned living quarters.

Which one would you explore first?

And what do you think you would find?

Things to Think About

  1. Why do you think engineers decided to build Camp Century under the ice instead of on top of it?
  2. What was the biggest engineering problem with Camp Century?
  3. Would you be comfortable living underground in a place like Camp Century for six months?
  4. If you could explore one abandoned place anywhere in the world, where would you choose?
  5. What is one machine or invention you would bring with you to explore Camp Century today?

Challenge

Design your own research base for an extreme environment.

You could build it:

  • under the Arctic ice
  • on the ocean floor
  • inside a mountain
  • in a desert
  • on the Moon
  • on Mars

Think about where people would sleep, eat, get electricity, obtain water and escape in an emergency.

The stranger the location, the better.
